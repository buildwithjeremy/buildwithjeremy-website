Every owner I work with hits this question eventually, usually around the point where the
software bill stops feeling like a rounding error. Someone suggests building something custom.
Someone else says that sounds expensive and risky. Both of them are right, in different
situations, and the argument usually goes nowhere because nobody has defined what they are
actually comparing.

So let me lay out the whole thing the way I would if we were sitting in your office.

## What you are actually choosing between

The phrase "build vs buy" makes it sound like two doors. In practice there are five routes, and
most owners only get pitched two of them:

1. **Vertical SaaS** — software built for your specific trade. Jobber, Housecall Pro,
   ServiceTitan, and their equivalents in every industry.
2. **The no-code stack** — Airtable or Notion for the data, Zapier or Make for the automation,
   Softr or similar for the interface. You assemble it yourself out of rented parts.
3. **App builders** — Bubble, Glide, and friends. One platform, real custom logic, visual editor.
4. **A dev shop or freelancer** — bespoke software built to your spec, hourly or by project.
5. **Productized custom** — a custom system with the scope, price, and go-live date fixed before
   anything starts.

Routes 1 through 3 rent you capability. Routes 4 and 5 build it. That is the actual distinction,
and it matters more than the label.

## What are the pros and cons of building custom software versus buying off-the-shelf SaaS?

**Buying is fast, cheap to start, and somebody else's problem to maintain.** You are live this
afternoon. The vendor employs people who think about nothing but that software. When it breaks,
it is their weekend, not yours. For most jobs, most of the time, this is simply correct and I
will tell you so.

The costs show up later, and they show up in three specific places.

**You adapt to the tool.** Every off-the-shelf product encodes assumptions about how a business
like yours works. Where those assumptions match, you get years of value for very little money.
Where they do not, you get a workaround — a spreadsheet beside the system, a naming convention
everybody has to remember, a step where someone re-types something that already exists
elsewhere. Workarounds are invisible on the invoice and expensive in practice.

**Per-seat pricing is a tax on growth.** Your software bill rises every time you hire. That is
fine at five people and it is a real line item at twenty-five. Notice what it does to the
incentive: the tool gets more expensive precisely as you succeed, which is exactly backwards
from how a good input should behave.

**The missing 20% has no exit.** This is the one owners underestimate. When a tool does not do
something, there is usually no setting for it, no add-on, and — critically — no export that
would let you do it somewhere else. The report you want does not exist and the data needed to
build it yourself is not available in a usable shape. You are not blocked by a feature gap. You
are blocked by a data gap, and those do not resolve with a support ticket.

**Building inverts all three.** The system matches how you actually work, the price stops
tracking headcount, and the data is yours in a form you can query. In exchange you take on two
things you did not have before: a project, and something to own afterwards.

The project part is the one people fear and it is genuinely the smaller risk now. The part worth
thinking hard about is ownership. Custom software is not a purchase, it is a small ongoing
responsibility — someone has to host it, patch it, and change it when the business changes. Any
honest answer to "should I build" includes a plan for who does that.

## When should a small business build custom software instead of using off-the-shelf tools?

Here is the test I actually use. Three questions, and you want a clear yes on at least one.

**Is the workflow you would automate the thing that makes you money?** Not payroll. Not
bookkeeping. The specific sequence your business runs on — how a lead becomes a scheduled job
becomes an invoice becomes a repeat customer. If your edge lives in that sequence, then bending
it to fit generic software is filing down the thing that makes you different. If your edge is
elsewhere, buy the standard tool and get on with it.

**Does your software bill grow every time you grow?** Count what you pay per seat, per record,
per contact, per automation run. If that number scales with your success rather than with the
value you get, you are renting on terms that get worse over time.

**Are you paying three or more tools to each do part of one job?** Not three tools doing three
different jobs — that is normal and fine. Three tools doing one job, with someone moving data
between them. That is the pattern where custom pays, because you are already paying for the
integration; you are just paying for it in hours instead of dollars.

If all three are no, you do not need custom software. You might need to use what you have
properly, which is cheaper and less exciting.

## How do I know if my business has outgrown Airtable and Zapier?

I have a lot of affection for this stack. It is the fastest way to find out what your process
actually is, and I would rather someone prototype in Airtable than spend three months writing a
spec for something nobody has tested.

You have outgrown it when three things are true at once.

**You are paying to raise limits.** Record caps, task caps, seat counts. When you are upgrading
tiers to keep the same workflow running, the pricing model has stopped matching your usage.

**One person is the designated knower.** There is somebody who understands how the automations
fit together, and when they are on holiday, changes wait. That is not a personnel problem, it is
an architecture problem — the system's logic lives in a place that cannot be read by anyone else.

**Failures are silent.** This is the real one. Zapier will email you when an automation errors.
The failure that hurts is the one that does not error — a record arrives in a slightly different
shape, does not match the filter, and is quietly skipped. Nothing is broken, so nothing tells
you. You find out from a customer three weeks later, and by then it has happened forty more times.

That third signal is the line. Once the connective tissue between your tools has become
load-bearing, it needs to be something somebody owns and can test, and no amount of additional
Zaps gets you there.

## How do service businesses cut software costs without losing functionality?

Before you consider building anything, do this. It takes an afternoon and it is free.

List every tool you pay for, its monthly cost, and the one job it does. Write the job in a short
sentence — if it takes two sentences, the tool is doing two jobs and that is worth knowing.

Almost every stack I have seen has the same shape: two or three tools with overlapping
functionality that arrived at different times for different reasons, and one or two doing
something genuinely irreplaceable. **Cancel the overlaps first.** That is free money and it
requires no project.

Then add a second column: roughly how many hours a month does your team spend moving information
between these tools, or re-entering something that already exists somewhere else? You do not
need a precise number. You need to know whether it is closer to two hours or twenty.

Now you have the real comparison. Not "custom software versus my SaaS bill," but "custom
software versus my SaaS bill plus the hours." That second number is usually the larger one and
it is the one nobody puts on a spreadsheet.

## What about the hybrid nobody pitches?

The framing that gets sold is all-or-nothing: keep your tools, or replace everything. The
version that actually works most often is neither.

Keep the tools that genuinely fit. Your accounting software is fine. Your email is fine. Build
custom only for the part that is yours — the workflow, the data model, the interface your team
lives in all day — and let it talk to the rest.

This is usually the right first step, and it is a smaller decision than it sounds. You are not
betting the business on a rebuild. You are building the one piece nobody sells, and connecting
it to the pieces plenty of people sell well.

## How I would decide if it were my business

Honestly? I would start by not building anything.

I would do the tool inventory. I would cancel the overlaps. I would find out whether the pain is
a tooling problem or a process problem, because a surprising amount of what looks like bad
software is actually an undecided process, and building custom software around an undecided
process just makes the confusion permanent and expensive.

Then, if the three questions above still came back yes — the workflow is the business, the bill
scales with growth, several tools are doing one job — I would build. Not everything. The one
piece that is actually mine, with a fixed scope and a date, and a clear answer to who owns it
afterwards.

And if it came back no, I would use the tools I have properly and spend the money on something
that grows revenue instead. That answer is less interesting and it is right more often than the
other one.

The question is never really "build or buy." It is "where does this cost land, and who carries
it." Once you can answer that, the decision usually makes itself.
